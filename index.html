01 The problem

The mCDR ecosystem is diverse.
MRV should be unified.

Meeting climate objectives requires not just reducing emissions, but actively removing carbon already in the atmosphere. Marine carbon dioxide removal (mCDR) offers some of the most cost effective and scalable pathways to close the gap.

mCDR spans nature-based pathways — biomass growth, ocean fertilization, blue carbon — and engineered pathways — ocean alkalinity enhancement, electrochemical capture, biomass burial. All are scaling. All face the same bottleneck: buyers, regulators, and insurers need direct, in-situ evidence that carbon is removed and stays removed.

Current measuring reporting and verification (MRV) protocols are pathway-specific: discrete water sampling, satellite proxies, bespoke biogeochemical models. Sparse, delayed, expensive, and incomparable across approaches. The gap between what projects claim and what can be verified is the single largest risk to market growth.

Diverse pathways, common physics

Every carbon transformation — whether driven by photosynthesis, mineral dissolution, or microbial metabolism — produces measurable heat and electron-transfer signals. Current MRV ignores this common ground.

Sparse data, high stakes

A handful of discrete water samples per square kilometre per quarter cannot resolve the fate of megatonne-scale carbon removal. Uncertainty feeds discount rates that destroy project economics.

Costly MRV

Monitoring, reporting, and verification can consume 30–50% of project revenue. Scaling requires cheaper, faster, more credible approaches — and that starts with better sensors.

02 The approach

Pathway-agnostic by design.
Energy as the universal signal.

Every carbon transformation in the ocean — whether driven by photosynthesis in biomass growth, mineral dissolution in ocean alkalinity enhancement, or microbial metabolism in blue carbon habitats — involves a change in heat flow. This is the thermal (enthalpic) signature: a universal signal that cuts across pathways.

Enthalpic's platform detects these signatures directly in the environment, in real time. Because we measure the underlying reaction energetics — not pathway-specific proxies — our approach is inherently pathway-agnostic. The same sensor stack works for OAE, biomass burial, blue carbon, and beyond. Different pathways, same physics, different signal processing.

03 Our technology

Quantum-enabled sensing
for carbon transformation.

Quantum-enabled thermal sensing

Our core sensor technology uses quantum-sensitive materials to detect minute heat signatures from carbon transformations. Developed in partnership with Fraunhofer Institute (Start-a-Factory, Berlin). Provides real-time, in-situ evidence of carbon cycling activity at sensitivities conventional systems cannot reach.

Microfluidic sampling

Low-power, in-situ microfluidic platforms for automated collection and analysis of porewater and seawater samples. Enables high-resolution biogeochemical profiling across depth and time.

AI uncertainty modeling

Probabilistic models that fuse sensor data with physical priors to quantify uncertainty in carbon permanence and transformation pathway attribution. Pathway-agnostic by design — applies equally to OAE, biomass, blue carbon, and electrochemical approaches.

Quantum sensor and microfluidic technology developed in partnership with Fraunhofer Institute (Start-a-Factory, Berlin). AI modeling is in early prototyping.

05 Why now

Measurement infrastructure
is the bottleneck.

Biomass burial pilots are moving from research to deployment. Ocean alkalinity enhancement are scaling. Blue carbon credits are trading. Corporate buyers, Goverments' funding, and VCs are backing multi-million-tonne projects across the full mCDR spectrum. But buyers are clear: MRV needs to catch up — and it needs to be comparable across pathways.

The current standard — pathway-specific grab sampling + bespoke models — cannot deliver the per-tonne verification that high-quality credits demand, nor the cross-pathway comparability that efficient markets require. Measurement infrastructure, not project pipeline, is becoming the binding constraint on market growth. Enthalpic is building the unified sensing layer to close that gap.

06 Status

Built at Carbon 13,
based at LEAP Berlin.

Enthalpic was founded by Alberto Robador, an assistant professor for geomicrobiology and environmental biogeochemistry at the University of Southern California (los Angeles, US), whose research sits at the intersection of microbial energetics, thermodynamics, and environmental sensing.

Alberto is part of the Carbon 13 venture-build programme and is developing Enthalpic from LEAP Berlin, within Berlin's Quantum Technology Hub, with ongoing conversations with mCDR project operators and developers, marine biogeochemistry research groups, and ecosystem monitoring partners.

The company is at an early stage, currently focused on sensor prototyping for biological and chemical carbon transformation signals, AI model development, and partnership development with field-deployment and research partners.
